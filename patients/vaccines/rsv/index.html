Vaccine information

RSV

What I want my patients to know about RSV: who needs the vaccine, when to get it, and what to expect.

Who
Everyone 75 and older, adults 50 to 74 with certain conditions, pregnant people at 32 to 36 weeks, and newborns
Doses
One, for now. It is not a yearly shot.
Best time
Late summer or early fall (August to October), but any time is fine
Cost
$0 with Medicare Part D
01

RSV is not just a baby’s cold.

RSV, respiratory syncytial virus, is best known for filling pediatric wards every winter. Fewer people know it does the same thing to older adults.

60,000–160,000hospital stays a year among U.S. adults 65 and older are linked to RSV, along with an estimated 6,000 to 10,000 deaths.

For a healthy 30-year-old, RSV usually feels like a cold. The trouble starts when it reaches the lungs of someone whose heart, lungs or immune system has less in reserve. Adults 75 and older account for nearly half of all adult RSV hospitalizations.

02

The name tells you what it does.

RSV stands for respiratory syncytial virus. The word comes from Greek: syn-, together, and kytos, a hollow vessel, the root behind every cyto- word in medicine. A syncytium is a group of cells fused into one. (Syn- is the same “together” as in syndrome.)

RSV carries a protein on its surface called the fusion protein, or F. The virus uses it to merge with the cells lining your airways and get inside. Infected cells then put that same protein on their own surface and start fusing with their healthy neighbors, merging into one large, misshapen cell with many nuclei.

Why that matters for your lungs. Your airways are lined with cells covered in tiny hairs called cilia. They beat in waves, sweeping mucus and trapped germs up and out. Think of it as your lungs’ escalator. These ciliated cells are exactly the ones RSV targets.

As infected cells fuse, die and slough off, the escalator stops. In lab studies of human airway lining, RSV stalled the cilia within hours and stripped away the ciliated cells within days. The smallest airways can then clog with mucus, dead cells and inflammation.

Repair takes longer than the illness. The lining regrows from cells underneath, but it is slow. In children studied after common respiratory viruses, normal cilia took 2 to 10 weeks to return. After bronchiolitis in infants, which RSV causes most often, the lining took an average of 13 to 17 weeks to look normal again. We have less data in adults, but the same process helps explain why a cough can hang on for weeks after the fever is gone, and why bacteria have an easier time during that window.

An open door for pneumonia. With the escalator stopped, bacteria that would normally be swept out can settle in. RSV also does something more direct: another of its proteins, called G, sits on infected cells and acts like a docking station for pneumococcus, the most common cause of bacterial pneumonia. In lab studies, contact with that protein also made the bacteria more aggressive, and seasonal waves of RSV are followed by more hospital admissions for serious pneumococcal infections. This is one reason I think of the RSV and pneumococcal vaccines as a pair.

03

Who should get it.

  • Everyone 75 and older.
  • Adults 50 to 74 with a condition that raises the risk of severe RSV:
    • Heart disease (high blood pressure alone does not count)
    • Lung disease, including COPD and asthma
    • Kidney failure or dialysis
    • Diabetes with complications, or treated with insulin or an SGLT2 medicine such as Jardiance or Farxiga
    • Chronic liver disease, or a chronic blood disorder such as sickle cell disease
    • A neurologic or muscle condition that makes it hard to clear your airway
    • Severe obesity (BMI 40 or higher)
    • A weakened immune system
    • Living in a nursing home, or frailty
  • Pregnant people at 32 to 36 weeks, September through January. Protection passes to the baby.
  • Newborns whose mother was not vaccinated in pregnancy get an antibody shot instead. Most babies need one or the other, not both. More on this below.

You do not need paperwork to prove a condition. Telling your clinician or pharmacist is enough.

04

One dose, for now.

CDC recommends a single dose, not a yearly shot. If you have already had one, you do not need another.

That does not mean protection lasts forever. In the longest study so far, of the GSK vaccine Arexvy, one dose cut RSV lung infections by about 83% in the first season, 56% in the second and 48% in the third. A second dose given a year later did not add much. Researchers are still working out whether and when a booster will help, especially for people 80 and older or who are frail. Until we know, one dose is the plan.

I think of it like a new roof: you put it on once, before the bad weather, and it keeps doing its job for years. Someday it may need work, but not every winter.

All three teach your immune system to recognize the F protein in its “before fusion” shape, so antibodies can block it before it fuses anything.

CDC does not prefer one over another. The best one is the one your pharmacy has in stock. You can get it at the same visit as your flu or COVID-19 shot, although you may feel a little more sore or achy afterward.

05

What to expect.

Most people have a sore arm, and some feel tired, achy or feverish for a day or two.

A rare risk I want you to know about. Early monitoring found a possible small increase in Guillain-Barré syndrome, a rare nerve condition that causes weakness, after two of the RSV vaccines. The data are still preliminary. CDC estimates that for adults 75 and older, and for higher-risk adults 60 to 74, the chance of being hospitalized with RSV is much greater than this risk.

06

Pregnancy and newborns.

There are two ways to protect a newborn, and most babies need only one:

  • A vaccine during pregnancy (Abrysvo) at 32 to 36 weeks, given September through January. Protection passes to the baby.
  • An antibody shot for the baby after birth, if the mother was not vaccinated.

Your obstetric and pediatric clinicians can help you choose.

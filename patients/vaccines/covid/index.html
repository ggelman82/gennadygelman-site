01

A new virus showed up in 2019.

In late 2019, doctors in Wuhan, China, saw a cluster of pneumonia cases with no clear cause. On December 31, 2019, Chinese authorities told the World Health Organization about it.

Scientists soon found a new virus. It was a coronavirus that had never infected people before. They named it SARS-CoV-2, and the illness it causes COVID-19.

Why “corona”? The word comes from Latin corona, meaning “crown” or “wreath.” Under an electron microscope, the spikes on these viruses look like a crown, or like the glowing ring around the sun. Virologists chose the name in 1968. Etymology of “corona” ↗

On March 11, 2020, the WHO declared it a pandemic. It was not the first time a new virus caught the world off guard. The 1918 flu pandemic was another one, and it is part of the story on my flu page.

Where the virus started is still studied. Most evidence points to an animal virus that spread to people. Read a brief review of the 2020 pandemic ↗

By mid-2026, countries had reported more than 7 million COVID-19 deaths to the WHO. The WHO estimates the real toll is far higher. Its 2026 report counts 22.1 million excess deaths from 2020 to 2023, meaning deaths above what would normally be expected.

In the United States, the CDC counted about 1.22 million deaths as of March 2025. The CDC updates this number, so check its site for the current total.

02

Why it makes you feel awful.

Symptoms usually start 2 to 14 days after exposure. Most people have mild illness. Common symptoms include fever or chills, cough, shortness of breath, sore throat, congestion, a new loss of taste or smell, fatigue, body aches, headache, nausea, and diarrhea. CDC: COVID-19 symptoms ↗

Most people feel better within a week or so. Some people have symptoms that last months or even years. That is called long COVID. Most people with long COVID improve after about 3 months. CDC: Long COVID ↗

03

Same sheriff, new wanted poster.

On my flu page I describe a vaccine as a wanted poster: it shows your immune system what the outlaw looks like before it rides into town. COVID-19 keeps changing its face, so each fall the vaccine is updated to match the strains going around. Last year’s poster still helps, but this year’s is a better likeness.

Older adults carry most of the risk. From October 2023 through August 2024, 70% of COVID-19 hospitalizations in the U.S. were in adults 65 and older.

CDC, the American Academy of Family Physicians and the Massachusetts Department of Public Health agree on these recommendations as of fall 2026.

Good to know

Under 65 and healthy? The vaccine is still recommended for you. The FDA labels list people under 65 who have a risk condition, so a pharmacy may ask about your health. You can simply tell them; no proof is required.

Just had COVID-19? You can wait up to three months before getting vaccinated.

06

What to expect.

A sore arm, tiredness, headache, muscle aches or a low fever for a day or so are common.

Heart warning signs

A rare inflammation of the heart, called myocarditis, has been seen mostly in young men after mRNA vaccines. Spacing doses at least 8 weeks apart lowers that risk. If you develop chest pain, shortness of breath or a racing heartbeat in the days after a shot, get checked.

The vaccine cannot give you COVID-19 and does not change your DNA.

07

Treatment works best early.

If you test positive and are at higher risk, call us early. CDC says COVID-19 treatment should start within 5 to 7 days after symptoms begin.

Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir with ritonavir) is a pill taken for five days. The FDA gave it full approval in 2023 for adults at high risk. Two recent national trials found it did not lower hospitalization or death in high-risk, mostly vaccinated groups. It did speed recovery. FDA Paxlovid prescribing information ↗

Check every medicine first

Paxlovid interacts with many medicines. The FDA has a boxed warning about serious interactions. For example, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin (cholesterol medicines) are usually paused during the five days. Tell us about everything you take, including over-the-counter products and herbal supplements.

A new option, for prevention only. In May 2026 the FDA approved ensitrelvir (Xocova) to prevent COVID-19 after close contact with someone who has it. It is for people 12 and older. It is not a treatment for people who are already sick.

08

Warning signs that mean get help now.

Get emergency medical care right away if you have any of these:

  • Persistent pain or pressure in the chest
  • New confusion
  • Trouble staying awake or being woken up
  • Pale, gray, or blue lips, face, or nail beds, depending on skin tone

If you have any of these, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. CDC: emergency warning signs ↗

Call us, too

Call us the same day if any of these happen. You do not need to be sure it is serious.

  • New or worse trouble breathing. If it is severe, or you are struggling to breathe while resting, call 911 instead.
  • Your symptoms get worse, or come back after you started to feel better
  • You cannot keep fluids down, or you are drinking much less than usual
  • You are still sick after about a week, or your symptoms are not getting better
  • You are worried about something that is not on this list

Calling us is never an overreaction. We would rather hear from you early.

09

Build your comfort plan.

For most people, symptoms last a few days and get better after about a week. Rest, drink plenty of fluids, and over-the-counter medicines may help with symptoms.

CDC's current guidance says to stay home and away from others until at least 24 hours after your symptoms are improving overall, and you have had no fever without using a fever-reducing medicine. CDC respiratory virus recommendations ↗

10

Medicines and home remedies.

I like home remedies. I just distinguish between “this makes me feel better” and “this treats COVID-19.” Those are very different claims.

Over-the-counter medicines may help with fever, aches, and other symptoms. CDC symptom guidance ↗

Over the counter does not mean risk-free

Acetaminophen can be dangerous in excessive doses. Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs can cause bleeding, affect the kidneys, and interact with medicines. Many cold and flu combination products already contain acetaminophen. Read every label, and check with us before you take anything new if you take Paxlovid.

What helps you feel better

  • Fluids: water, tea, broth, or electrolyte drinks. Small, frequent sips are easier when you feel nauseated.
  • Rest: sleep is not wasted time when you are sick. Your body is working on the infection.
  • Saline: warm salt-water gargles for a sore throat, and saline nasal spray for congestion.
  • Treat discomfort: you do not have to chase a modest fever if you are comfortable.

Things I would not spend much money on

  • Supplements sold as COVID-19 cures: there is no good evidence that they treat it.
  • Vitamin megadoses: correct real deficiencies. More is not automatically better.

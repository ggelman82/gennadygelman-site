For my patients

Cancer screening

Screening can prevent some cancers or find them before symptoms begin. But not every cancer has a useful screening test, and more testing isn't always better. Here's how to lower your risk, which screenings I recommend, which I don't, and why.

01

An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of screening

Screening finds cancer early. Prevention stops it from starting. About 4 in 10 cancer cases and nearly half of cancer deaths in US adults are linked to things we can change.

Don't smoke. If you do, quit.Smoking is behind nearly 1 in 5 cancer cases and about 3 in 10 cancer deaths, and not only lung cancer. Quitting helps at any age, and it's the single biggest thing you can do. Medicines and counseling make quitting much more likely, so let us help. Free support: 1-800-QUIT-NOW.
Drink less. None is best.Alcohol causes about 100,000 cancers and 20,000 cancer deaths in the US every year. It raises the risk of at least seven cancers, including breast, colon, liver, mouth and throat. Wine, beer and spirits are all the same here. For breast cancer, risk starts going up at about one drink a day or less.
Feed your gut for your colonFiber-rich foods like whole grains, beans, vegetables and fruit lower colon cancer risk. Processed meats like bacon, ham and hot dogs raise it, and red meat probably does too. Scientists are also finding that some gut bacteria make a toxin that damages DNA, and it's been linked to colon cancer in younger adults. That research is still young. Fiber may also help the good bacteria in your gut. But no probiotic supplement has been shown to prevent colon cancer.
Foods and habits for breast healthThe clearest link is alcohol: less is better. Staying active and avoiding weight gain after menopause lower risk, and so does breastfeeding. Eating more vegetables, especially leafy greens and orange or red foods like carrots, sweet potatoes and tomatoes, is linked to lower risk, though that evidence is weaker.
Respect the sunUltraviolet light is behind about 9 in 10 melanomas. Skip tanning beds entirely. Use shade, clothing, hats and sunscreen (SPF 30 or higher, reapplied), especially in midday sun. Protect children's skin: sunburns early in life count.
Get the HPV vaccineHPV causes almost all cervical cancers and many throat, anal and genital cancers. In Scotland, not one woman fully vaccinated at age 12 or 13 has developed cervical cancer. It's recommended for preteens, with catch-up vaccination through age 26. Two doses if you start before age 15, three doses if you start at 15 or older. If you're 27 to 45, ask us whether it makes sense for you.
Keep a healthy weight and moveExcess body weight is the second-biggest preventable cause of cancer after smoking, linked to at least 13 types. Regular activity lowers the risk of colon and breast cancer.
Get the hepatitis B vaccineLong-term hepatitis B infection can lead to liver cancer. The vaccine prevents the infection. If you're not sure whether you've had it, ask us.

Some people need more. A strong family history, an inherited gene change, past radiation or other risk factors can mean starting earlier, screening more often, or using a different test. And screening is for people without symptoms: if you notice something new, like bleeding, a lump or a cough that won't go away, don't wait for your next screening. Tell us.

Does cancer run in your family?

Some cancers run in families because of inherited gene changes, like BRCA or Lynch syndrome. Knowing that can change when and how you get screened, and it can matter for your relatives too.

04

Screenings I don't routinely do

More testing isn't always better. A screening test can cause harm: false alarms, extra scans, biopsies and sometimes surgery for something that never would have hurt you. For the tests below, the evidence doesn't show that the benefit is worth those risks for people without symptoms.

Recommended against

  • Ovarian cancerPelvic ultrasound or the CA-125 blood test. JAMA Patient Page ↗
  • Pancreatic cancerImaging or blood tests in people without symptoms. JAMA Patient Page ↗
  • Testicular cancerRoutine exams by a clinician or self-exams. USPSTF recommendation ↗
  • Thyroid cancerNeck ultrasound or exam in people without symptoms. JAMA Patient Page ↗

Not enough evidence either way

  • Skin cancerFull-body skin checks by a clinician. Get to know your own skin, and show us any spot that's new, changing, bleeding or looks different from the rest. JAMA Patient Page ↗
  • Oral cancerRoutine mouth exams by a clinician to look for cancer. Your dentist may still check your mouth at cleanings. USPSTF recommendation ↗
  • Bladder cancerUrine tests in people without symptoms. Blood in your urine is not normal: tell us. USPSTF recommendation ↗

What about liver cancer? We don't screen people at average risk for liver cancer. But we do test every adult for hepatitis C at least once, because untreated hepatitis C can lead to liver cancer, and today it can be cured with a few months of pills. JAMA Patient Page on hepatitis C screening ↗

These apply to people at average risk. If you're at higher risk, for example because of a family history of ovarian cancer or a known gene change, the plan is different, and we'll make it together.

05

Multi-cancer blood tests

You may have seen ads for blood tests like Galleri or Cancerguard that look for signs of dozens of cancers from a single blood draw. They are a real scientific advance, and many patients ask me about them. Here's where they stand.

Not FDA-approved yetThey're sold as lab-developed tests. In September 2026 an FDA advisory panel voted in favor of Galleri. The FDA hasn't made its decision yet.
Not proven to save livesIn a UK trial of about 142,000 people, Galleri found more cancers and fewer stage 4 cancers. It did not meet its main goal: fewer cancers found at stage 3 or 4 combined.
A positive result means more testingUsually CT, MRI or PET scans, sometimes a biopsy, to find where the signal comes from. Sometimes nothing is found.
A negative result isn't an all-clearThese tests miss many cancers. They're much better at finding cancers that are already advanced than early ones.
You usually pay out of pocketAbout $600 to $1,000 (Cancerguard $689, Galleri about $949). Follow-up scans after a positive result are billed separately.

My take

These tests aren't ready for prime time yet, but they're a good start. None of them beats the proven tests: a blood test is not better than a colonoscopy for colon cancer, a mammogram for breast cancer or a Pap test for cervical cancer. What they offer is a look at cancers we have no screening test for at all, like pancreatic, liver, uterine and ovarian cancer.

They're expensive, and the results aren't always conclusive. The blood draw is low risk, but the testing process can lead to unnecessary scans, biopsies, anxiety and expense. We don't yet know whether these tests help people live longer. Let's talk it through, and keep up with your regular screenings either way.

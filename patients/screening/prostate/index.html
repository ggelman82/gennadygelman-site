01

Common, and usually slow.

Other than skin cancer, prostate cancer is the most common cancer in men. Most of the time it grows slowly, and many men who have it will die of something else without ever being troubled by it. Some prostate cancers are aggressive and deadly. The PSA test cannot tell the two apart, which is why screening is a decision and not a blanket recommendation.

02

Screening helps only if you are likely to live at least another 10 years, because of how slowly most prostate cancers grow.

Two sets of guidelines. The ages 55 to 69 come from the US Preventive Services Task Force, whose update is on hold. The American Cancer Society suggests starting the conversation at 50 for men at average risk. The earlier ages for men at higher risk are from the American Cancer Society.

03

The test.

PSA is a protein made by the prostate. A small amount leaks into the blood, and a blood test measures it. Cancer can raise it. So can several harmless things:

  • An enlarged prostate, which most men get with age
  • A prostate or urinary infection
  • Ejaculation in the day or two before the test
  • A long bike ride, or a recent urinary procedure

Some medicines for an enlarged prostate or hair loss (finasteride, dutasteride) lower PSA by about half. Tell us if you take one.

If you choose testing, a normal result is usually repeated every 2 years, or every year if your PSA is 2.5 or higher. The blood test is the screening test. A finger exam of the prostate may be added.

04

The benefits and harms, in numbers.

Picture 1,000 men aged 55 to 69 who get PSA tests for about 13 years.

What happensHow many of the 1,000
Avoid dying of prostate cancer because of screeningAbout 1
Avoid cancer that spreads to the bones or elsewhereAbout 3
Get at least one high PSA result250 to 330. Most do not have cancer.
Are diagnosed with a cancer that would never have harmed themBetween 2 and 5 of every 10 cancers found by screening

The harm comes mostly from treatment, not from the blood test. Among men who have surgery, about 1 in 5 has long-term urine leakage and about 2 in 3 have long-term erection problems. After radiation, more than half have erection problems and up to 1 in 6 has lasting bowel symptoms.

That is why low-risk cancers are now usually watched and not treated right away, which avoids much of this harm.

How men decide. Men who choose testing usually say they would rather know and want every chance to avoid a deadly cancer. Men who decline usually say the chance of benefit is small and they want to avoid treatment side effects for a cancer that might never have bothered them. Both are reasonable. The American Cancer Society decision aid (PDF) walks through it.

05

What a high PSA means.

Your PSAChance that a biopsy would find cancer
Under 4Low, though not zero: about 15 in 100
4 to 10About 1 in 4
Over 10More than half

There is no single cutoff. Your age, the size of your prostate and how fast the number is changing all matter.

What happens next

StepWhat it is
Repeat the PSAUsually in a few weeks. PSA bounces around, and a second test is often lower.
See a urologistIf it stays high. An MRI of the prostate is often done first, to see whether a biopsy is needed and where to aim it.
BiopsySmall samples taken with a needle. About 1 in 100 men is hospitalized afterward, usually for infection. Blood in the semen for a few weeks is common and harmless.

If cancer is found

The next question is how aggressive it is. For low-risk cancer, the usual choice is active surveillance: regular PSA tests, exams and repeat MRI or biopsies, with treatment only if it starts to change. In one large trial, about half of the men being watched went on to treatment within 10 years. Cancer spread was more common with watching (about 6 in 100) than with surgery or radiation (2 to 3 in 100), and deaths from prostate cancer were rare in all groups.

06

If you are at higher risk.

  • Black men get prostate cancer more often, at younger ages, and are more than twice as likely to die of it.
  • A father or brother with prostate cancer raises your risk, more so if he was diagnosed before 65 or died of it.
  • A BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene change, or Lynch syndrome, in you or your family raises the risk of aggressive prostate cancer. Take the family history questionnaire.

For these men the benefit of testing is probably larger. Start the conversation with us earlier.

07

Screening is for men who feel fine.

A weak stream, getting up at night to urinate, or trouble starting are very common and are usually from an enlarged prostate, not cancer. They are still worth a visit, because they can be treated. See us promptly for:

  • Blood in your urine or semen
  • New bone pain, especially in the back or hips, that does not go away
  • Losing weight without trying
08

Lower your risk.

There is no proven way to prevent prostate cancer. A healthy weight, regular activity and not smoking are good bets for your overall health, and they lower the risk of several other cancers. See the prevention section on the main page.

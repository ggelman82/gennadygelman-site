Cancer screening

Colon and rectal cancer

What I want my patients to know about screening for colon and rectal cancer: who needs it, which test to pick, and what happens if a result comes back abnormal.

Who
Adults 45 to 75 at average risk
Test
A colonoscopy or a home stool test
How often
Every 10 years, 3 years or 1 year, depending on the test
Cost
$0 for screening with Medicare and most insurance
01

The cancer we can stop before it starts.

Most colon and rectal cancers begin as a polyp, a small growth on the inner lining of the bowel. Polyps are common and most never turn into cancer. The ones that do usually take years. That slow start is our opening: find the polyp, remove it, and the cancer it might have become never happens.

So this screening does two jobs. It finds cancer early, when it is far easier to treat. And it can prevent cancer altogether.

91%of people are alive five years after a colon or rectal cancer found before it has spread. Once it has spread to distant organs, that drops to about 15%.

02

Who should be screened, and when.

Your ageWhat I recommend
Under 45No routine screening at average risk. Start earlier if you are at higher risk (section 6), and tell us about symptoms at any age (section 7).
45 to 75Get screened, and stay on schedule.
76 to 85A personal decision. It depends on your health, your past results and what you want. If you have been screened regularly with normal results, the benefit of more testing is small.
Over 85Stop screening.

Average risk means no colon or rectal cancer or precancerous polyps in your past, no inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease), no close relative with colon or rectal cancer, and no inherited syndrome such as Lynch syndrome.

03

Your options.

There is more than one good test. They differ in how often you do them, what they ask of you, and how well they find polyps before they become cancer.

TestHow oftenWhat it involvesWhat it finds
ColonoscopyEvery 10 yearsA bowel prep the day before, sedation, and a ride home. A doctor looks at the whole colon with a thin, flexible camera.The most thorough test. Polyps are removed during the same procedure, which is how it prevents cancer.
FIT (stool blood test)Every yearA small stool sample you collect at home and mail in. No prep, no diet change.Looks for hidden blood. One test finds about 7 in 10 cancers and about 1 in 4 advanced polyps. Doing it every year is what makes it work.
Stool DNA test (Cologuard)Every 3 yearsA full stool sample collected at home with a kit that is shipped to you and mailed back.Looks for blood and abnormal DNA. Finds more than 9 in 10 cancers and about 4 in 10 advanced polyps. More false alarms than FIT: about 1 in 10 people with nothing serious test positive.
CT colonography (“virtual colonoscopy”)Every 5 yearsA bowel prep, then a CT scan. No sedation.Finds cancers and larger polyps. Uses a small dose of radiation and can turn up unrelated findings elsewhere in the abdomen.

Which one do I recommend? A colonoscopy or a home stool test, whichever you will actually do and keep doing. A colonoscopy is the most complete and you may not need another for 10 years. A stool test is easier and has no procedure risk, but it only works if you repeat it on schedule and follow up a positive result.

What about the blood test? A blood test for colon cancer (Shield) is now FDA approved. It finds most cancers but misses most precancerous polyps and more of the earliest cancers, so it cannot prevent cancer the way the tests above can. The American Cancer Society says to use it only if you are unwilling to do a stool test or a colonoscopy. If that is you, talk with us: a blood test is better than no screening.

04

Benefits and risks.

The benefit is fewer cancers and fewer deaths. Screening is a main reason colon and rectal cancer has been falling for decades in older adults, the group that gets screened the most.

The risks are small, and they are real.

TestWhat can go wrong
ColonoscopySerious bleeding in about 15 of every 10,000 procedures, and a tear in the bowel wall (perforation) in about 3 of every 10,000. Both are more likely with age and when a polyp is removed. The prep is unpleasant, and you lose a day to it.
Stool testsThe test itself cannot hurt you. The risks are a false alarm, which leads to a colonoscopy that finds nothing, and a false reassurance, when the test misses something. That is why the schedule matters.
Any testNo test is perfect. A normal result lowers your risk a great deal. It does not make it zero.
05

What an abnormal result means.

A positive stool test

A positive FIT or Cologuard does not mean you have cancer. Most people with a positive result do not. In one large study, about 3 in 100 people with a positive FIT had cancer. Many more have polyps, and some have nothing at all.

It does mean you need a colonoscopy to find out. Do not repeat the stool test to “double-check.” A second, negative test does not cancel the first.

Do not put it off. Aim to have the colonoscopy within a few months. In that same study, people who waited more than about 10 months were more likely to have cancer, and to have it at a later stage.

What it costs. A colonoscopy after a positive stool test is part of your screening, and the law treats it that way. Most private insurance plans have had to cover it with no out-of-pocket cost since 2022, and Medicare since 2023. With Medicare, if a polyp is removed you may owe a share of the cost; that share is being phased out and ends in 2030. Plans differ, so check yours before the procedure.

Polyps found on a colonoscopy

Polyps are removed and sent to a lab. Most are harmless or precancerous, and removing them is the whole point. What changes is your schedule: depending on how many there were, how large, and what kind, your next colonoscopy may be due in 3 to 10 years. The doctor who did the procedure will tell you when.

If cancer is found

We move quickly to get you imaging and a visit with a surgeon or cancer specialist. Cancers found by screening are more often early, and early colon cancer is very treatable.

06

If you are at higher risk.

Some people should start earlier, screen more often, or go straight to colonoscopy. Tell us if any of these apply to you.

Your situationWhat usually changes
A parent, sibling or child had colon or rectal cancer, or an advanced polyp, before age 60. Or two of them did at any age.Colonoscopy every 5 years, starting at age 40 or 10 years before the youngest relative’s diagnosis, whichever comes first.
One parent, sibling or child was diagnosed at 60 or olderStart at 40 instead of 45. Any of the usual tests is fine.
You have had precancerous polyps or colon cancerFollow-up colonoscopies on a schedule set by your gastroenterologist. Stool tests are no longer the right test for you.
Ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease of the colonEarlier and more frequent colonoscopies, managed with your gastroenterologist.
Lynch syndrome, familial polyposis, or a strong family pattern of cancerA plan built for you, often starting in your 20s. Take the family history questionnaire.
07

Screening is for people who feel fine.

If you have symptoms, you need an evaluation now, at any age, even if your last screening was normal. Do not wait for your next one, and do not use a home stool test to check a symptom.

  • Blood in your stool, or stool that is black and tarry
  • A change in your bowel habits that lasts more than a few weeks
  • Belly pain or cramping that does not go away
  • Losing weight without trying
  • Low iron or anemia with no clear cause

Most of the time the cause is not cancer. It still needs to be checked.

08

Lower your risk.

More than half of colon and rectal cancers are linked to things we can change. Screening is one part. The rest is on the main cancer screening page: fiber and less processed meat, less alcohol, not smoking, and a healthy weight and regular activity.

If you remember nothing else

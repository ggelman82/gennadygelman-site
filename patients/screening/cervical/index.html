Cancer screening

Cervical cancer

What I want my patients to know about Pap and HPV tests: when to start, how often, when you can stop, and what an abnormal result means.

Who
Anyone with a cervix, ages 21 to 65
Test
A Pap test, an HPV test, or both
How often
Every 3 or 5 years, depending on the test
Prevention
The HPV vaccine

Key points

21 to 65? Stay on schedule.Every 3 or 5 years, depending on the test. Even if you had the HPV vaccine.
An abnormal result is common and is rarely cancer.It means we look closer. Most changes are found years before they could turn into cancer.
You can stop at 65 only with normal recent results.Not just because of your age.
Bleeding after sex or after menopause? Do not wait.That needs a visit, not a screening test.
01

A cancer we know how to prevent.

Almost all cervical cancer is caused by HPV, a very common virus passed by skin-to-skin sexual contact. Most infections clear on their own. When one lingers for years, it can slowly change the cells of the cervix. Screening finds those changes while they are still precancer, and treating them prevents the cancer.

It works. Screening has cut cervical cancer in the US by more than half since the mid-1970s. Most cervical cancers today are found in people who were never screened or had not been screened in a long time.

02

Who should be screened, and when.

Your ageWhat I recommend
Under 21No screening, whether or not you are sexually active.
21 to 29A Pap test every 3 years.
30 to 65One of three: an HPV test every 5 years, a Pap test every 3 years, or both together every 5 years.
Over 65You can stop if your recent results were normal (see below) and you have no history of a high-grade precancer.
After a hysterectomyIf your cervix was removed and you never had a high-grade precancer or cervical cancer, you do not need screening. If you still have your cervix, keep screening.

When you can stop

Age 65 is not enough by itself. You need a recent record of normal tests: 3 normal Pap tests in a row, or 2 normal HPV tests or co-tests in a row, within the past 10 years, with the most recent in the past 5. If you were treated for a high-grade precancer, keep screening for at least 20 years afterward, even past 65.

If you are over 65 and are not sure when you were last screened, ask us. You may need to catch up. A new partner after 65 is not a reason to restart if you stopped properly.

Two sets of guidelines. I follow the US Preventive Services Task Force schedule above. Its update has been on hold since 2025. The American Cancer Society updated its own guideline in December 2025: start at 25, use an HPV test every 5 years as the first choice, and have normal HPV tests at both 60 and 65 before stopping. Either approach is reasonable, and I am glad to talk through which fits you.

Had the HPV vaccine? You still need screening. The vaccine prevents most cervical cancers, not all of them.

04

Benefits and risks.

The benefit is large: far fewer cervical cancers and deaths, because precancer is found and treated first.

RiskWhat it means
False alarmsMany HPV infections and mild cell changes go away without treatment. Finding them can mean extra tests and worry for something that would have cleared.
Follow-up proceduresA closer exam with a biopsy (colposcopy) can cause cramping, spotting and, rarely, infection.
Treatment and later pregnanciesRemoving precancer from the cervix is linked to a somewhat higher chance of preterm birth later. That is one reason we watch mild changes instead of treating them right away, especially in younger women.
WorryA positive HPV test can be upsetting. See the next section for what it does and does not mean.
05

What an abnormal result means.

An abnormal Pap or a positive HPV test is common, and it almost never means cancer. It means we need to look more closely or check again.

A positive HPV test

Most adults who have been sexually active get HPV at some point. It can stay silent for years, so a positive test says nothing about when you got it or from whom. It does not mean you or your partner were unfaithful. Most infections clear within a couple of years.

What happens next

Next stepWhen we use it
Repeat the test in 1 yearFor lower-risk results, such as a positive HPV test with a normal Pap, or mild cell changes. Many clear by then.
ColposcopyFor higher-risk results. A clinician looks at the cervix with a magnifier and takes one or more tiny samples. It is done in the office and takes about 15 minutes.
Watch, or treatMild precancer is usually watched, because it often goes away. High-grade precancer is removed, most often with a short office procedure called a LEEP.

What we recommend depends on this result and on your past results, so the plan can differ between two people with the same test. The one wrong move is not following up.

06

If you are at higher risk.

You need a different plan, usually more frequent testing that continues past 65, if any of these apply:

  • A weakened immune system, including HIV or medicines that suppress immunity
  • A past high-grade precancer or cervical cancer
  • Your mother took DES (diethylstilbestrol) while pregnant with you

The self-collected test is not the right choice in these situations.

07

Screening is for people who feel fine.

If you have symptoms, you need an exam now, even if your last test was normal.

  • Bleeding after sex
  • Bleeding between periods, or any bleeding after menopause
  • Vaginal discharge that is new, watery, bloody or foul-smelling
  • Pelvic pain, or pain during sex

Most of the time the cause is not cancer. It still needs to be checked.

08

Lower your risk.

The HPV vaccine prevents most cervical cancers and is the best protection we have. Not smoking matters too: smoking makes it harder for the body to clear HPV.

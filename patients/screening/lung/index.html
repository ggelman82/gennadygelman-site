Cancer screening

Lung cancer

What I want my patients to know about the yearly low-dose CT scan: who qualifies, what it can and cannot do, and what it means if the scan finds a spot.

01

What raises your risk, and what lowers it.

Lung cancer causes more cancer deaths in the US than any other cancer, mostly because it is usually found late. There are often no symptoms until it has spread. A yearly low-dose CT scan can find it early in the people most likely to get it.

What raises your risk

  • Smoking cigarettes. This is by far the biggest cause. The more you smoked and the longer, the higher the risk.
  • Heavy secondhand smoke over many years
  • Radon in your home. It is the leading cause in people who never smoked. You can test for it with an inexpensive kit, and a high level can be fixed. More on radon.
  • Work exposure to asbestos, diesel exhaust or similar
  • A parent, sibling or child with lung cancer
  • COPD or emphysema, or scarring of the lungs

Only your age and smoking history decide whether you qualify for a screening scan. Tell us about the others anyway, because they matter when we decide together.

What lowers it

Quitting smoking is the single most effective thing you can do, at any age, and it helps your heart and lungs right away. Medicines and counseling make quitting much more likely. Ask us, or call 1-800-QUIT-NOW for free support.

Test your home for radon, whether or not you ever smoked.

02

Screening is for people who feel fine.

If you have symptoms, you need an evaluation now, whether or not you qualify for screening.

  • A cough that does not go away, or a change in a long-standing cough
  • Coughing up blood
  • Chest pain, or shortness of breath that is new
  • Hoarseness that lasts
  • Losing weight without trying
03

Who should be screened, and when.

You qualify if all three are true:

  • You are 50 to 80 years old
  • You have smoked at least 20 pack-years
  • You smoke now, or you quit within the past 15 years

Counting pack-years

Multiply the packs of cigarettes you smoked per day by the number of years you smoked.

How muchFor how longPack-years
1 pack a day20 years20
2 packs a day10 years20
1½ packs a dayA little over 13 years20
Half a pack a day40 years20

Only cigarettes count toward pack-years. Cigars, pipes and vaping are not part of the screening criteria.

When to stop

  • You turn 81
  • It has been 15 years since you quit
  • You develop a health problem that would rule out lung surgery or that seriously limits how long you are likely to live

Two sets of guidelines. I follow the US Preventive Services Task Force, and so do Medicare and most insurers. Since 2023 the American Cancer Society has recommended continuing yearly scans no matter how long ago you quit, because the risk never drops back to that of someone who never smoked. If you quit more than 15 years ago and are worried, talk with us, and check your coverage first.

Never smoked? Screening is not recommended, even with a family history. The risks of the scan outweigh the benefit when the chance of cancer is low.

04

The test.

A low-dose CT scan takes a few minutes. You lie on a table that slides through a ring-shaped scanner and hold your breath for a few seconds. There are no needles, no dye, and nothing to prepare.

QuestionAnswer
How much radiation?A small dose: less than, or about the same as, what you get from natural background radiation in a year. A regular chest CT uses several times more.
Why not a chest X-ray?Chest X-rays do not lower deaths from lung cancer. They miss small tumors.
What happens first?A visit to talk through the benefits and risks is required before your first scan. That is this conversation.
Where?At a center that does lung screening regularly and follows a standard system for reading the scans.
05

Benefits and risks.

The benefit: in two large trials, people at high risk who had yearly scans were about 16% to 25% less likely to die of lung cancer than those who did not.

The risks:

RiskWhat it means
False alarmsWith current reading standards, about 1 in 8 first scans finds something that needs follow-up and turns out not to be cancer. It is less common on later scans, when there are older pictures to compare.
Procedures for a false alarmIn the largest US trial, fewer than 2 in 100 people screened had a biopsy or surgery for something that was not cancer. Serious complications were rare.
OverdiagnosisA small share of the cancers found, about 6 in 100, would never have caused harm.
Other findingsThe scan sees your heart, thyroid and upper abdomen too. Some findings are useful, such as calcium in the heart arteries. Others lead to more tests for something harmless.
RadiationSmall with each scan. It adds up over many years, which is one reason screening is limited to people at high risk.
06

What it means if the scan finds a spot.

A small spot on the lung is called a nodule. Nodules are very common, and most are not cancer. They are usually old scars or small areas of inflammation. What we do depends on the size and look of the nodule.

Your report saysWhat happens next
Negative or benign (Lung-RADS 1 or 2)Come back in a year.
Probably benign (Lung-RADS 3)Another low-dose CT in 6 months to make sure nothing is growing.
Suspicious (Lung-RADS 4)A sooner CT, usually in 3 months, or a PET scan or biopsy, depending on how it looks. You would see a lung specialist.

Waiting a few months for a repeat scan can be hard. It is the safe approach: a nodule that does not grow is almost never cancer, and watching it avoids a biopsy you do not need.

If cancer is found, we move quickly to get you to a lung cancer team. Cancers found by screening are more often early, when surgery or focused radiation can cure them.

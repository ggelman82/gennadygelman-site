Memory and brain health

Some forgetfulness is a normal part of getting older. Some is not, and finding it early matters. Here is how to tell the difference, how we check memory, what you can do to lower your risk, and where families can find help.

7.4 millionAmericans 65 and older living with Alzheimer's disease in 2026, about 1 in 9
45%of dementia worldwide may be prevented or delayed by tackling 14 risk factors
13 millionAmericans caring, unpaid, for someone with dementia

Alzheimer's Association, 2026 Facts and Figures; Lancet Commission on dementia, 2024

Key points

Forgetting a name is normal.A change from how you used to be that gets in the way of daily life is worth bringing up.
Sudden confusion needs care right away.Call 911 for stroke signs.
We check memory from age 65, sooner if anyone is concerned.Many causes are treatable, so we look for those first.
Nearly half of dementia may be preventable.Move every day, keep blood pressure, cholesterol and sugar in range, protect your hearing, stay connected.
Blood tests and the newer medicines are for people with symptoms.They are not for predicting the future in someone who feels fine.
01

Normal aging, or something more?

Everyone forgets things. What matters is a change from how you used to be, and whether it gets in the way of daily life. Family members often notice before the person does.

Usually normal agingWorth bringing up with us
Forgetting a name, then remembering it laterForgetting recent conversations or events, asking the same question again and again
Sometimes searching for the right wordOften losing words, or losing track of a conversation
Misplacing keys and retracing your steps to find themPutting things in odd places, or thinking someone took them
Missing a bill payment onceTrouble keeping up with bills, a recipe or a plan you used to handle easily
Forgetting the date, then working it outLosing track of the season or year, or getting lost in a familiar place
Needing help with a new phone or appTrouble with familiar tasks, like driving a known route or using the microwave
Making a bad decision now and thenNew poor judgment with money, or falling for scams
Feeling tired of busy social plansPulling away from hobbies and people, or new changes in mood or personality

Sudden confusion is different. Confusion that comes on over hours or days, or comes with weakness, trouble speaking or a drooping face, needs care right away. Call 911 for stroke signs. Infections and medicine side effects can also cause sudden confusion, especially in older adults.

02

How we check memory

A memory check is a standard part of your Medicare Annual Wellness Visit or yearly physical starting at age 65. From age 50, we do one sooner if you, your family or we have concerns. Many offices use a paper test. We use a short tablet check from Linus Health: you remember a few words and draw a clock with a digital pen.

The tablet records not just the finished drawing but how you drew it: pauses, the order of your strokes, and speed. That can pick up small changes a paper test misses. It takes a few minutes, and it is not a diagnosis. A result that is not normal means we look more closely.

03

If the check is not normal

Many things besides dementia can affect memory, and some are very treatable. We start by looking for those.

  • Look for treatable causes. We review your medicines (some sleep, allergy and bladder pills affect memory) and check blood tests such as thyroid and vitamin B12. We ask about depression, sleep, hearing, vision and alcohol.
  • Do a longer memory test. A longer test, such as the MoCA, looks at memory, attention, language and planning in more detail.
  • Look for the cause. An MRI or CT can look for strokes and other causes. A blood test can show whether Alzheimer's changes are likely (see below).
  • See a specialist if needed. A neurologist or memory clinic can confirm the diagnosis and talk about treatment.

Why find it early? Early diagnosis lets you treat what can be treated, plan legal and money matters while you can make your own choices, and, for Alzheimer's, consider newer medicines that only help in the early stages.

04

Alzheimer's blood tests and the APOE gene

The pTau217 blood test

Alzheimer's leaves a trail in the blood. A protein called pTau217 rises when amyloid, the sticky protein of Alzheimer's, builds up in the brain. Until recently, checking for amyloid took a PET scan or a spinal tap. Now a regular blood draw can do it.

Who it is forPeople 55 and older who have memory or thinking symptoms. The newest FDA-cleared version, from Roche, was cleared in August 2026 for use in primary care as well as by specialists.
Who it is not forPeople without symptoms. A positive result in someone who feels fine does not tell us if or when memory problems will start, and there is no approved treatment for that situation yet.
What the result meansResults come back negative (Alzheimer's is unlikely to be the cause), positive (amyloid is likely present) or intermediate (unclear; we may need a scan or a spinal tap).
What it does not doA positive result is not a diagnosis by itself. We put it together with your memory testing, history and other results. It also does not tell us how fast things will change.

Should I ask for one? If you have memory symptoms, it is a reasonable question, and it matters most if you are thinking about the newer Alzheimer's medicines. Insurance coverage varies, so we will check before ordering.

The APOE gene

Everyone has two copies of a gene called APOE, one from each parent. It comes in three common types: 2, 3 and 4. Type 4 (APOE4) is the strongest common gene linked to Alzheimer's.

Your APOE resultHow commonWhat it means
No copies of APOE4About 3 in 4 peopleAverage risk. You can still get Alzheimer's.
One copyAbout 1 in 4Roughly 2 to 3 times the usual risk. Most people with one copy never get Alzheimer's.
Two copiesAbout 2 in 100Much higher risk, often with earlier symptoms. Many, but not all, develop Alzheimer's.
When we test for itBefore starting Leqembi or Kisunla. People with APOE4, especially two copies, are more likely to get brain swelling or bleeding from these medicines, so the result helps weigh benefit against risk.
When we usually don'tTo predict your future if you have no symptoms. The result can't say whether you will get Alzheimer's, and the advice is the same either way: work on the risk factors below.
If you already knowSome people learn their APOE type from a consumer DNA test. If that happened to you, talk with us. A genetic counselor can help you understand it, including what it could mean for your children.
Before you testFederal law (GINA) keeps health insurers and employers from using genetic results against you, but it does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance. Think about that first.
05

Lower your risk

In 2024 an international group of experts, the Lancet Commission on dementia, named 14 things that raise the risk of dementia and can be changed. Together, they may account for nearly half of all dementia. It is never too early or too late to start.

What helps most

Move every dayBrisk walking counts. Aim for 150 minutes a week, plus some strength work.
Know your numbersKeep blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and blood sugar in range. Take the medicines that do it.
Protect your hearing and visionGet them checked, and wear your hearing aids and glasses.
Stay connected and keep learningTime with people, a class, a new skill, music or volunteering all help.
Eat mostly plantsVegetables, beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish, as in a Mediterranean-style diet.
Sleep wellAim for 7 to 8 hours. Loud snoring or stopping breathing at night can mean sleep apnea, which we can treat.
Don't smoke, and go easy on alcoholQuitting at any age helps. Less alcohol is better for the brain.
Protect your headHelmets, seat belts, and a home set up to prevent falls.
Get the shingles vaccineShingrix is two shots, recommended from age 50. Besides preventing shingles, it may lower dementia risk. In Wales, people who got an older shingles vaccine were about 20% less likely to be diagnosed with dementia over the next 7 years, and studies of Shingrix point the same way. 06

Treatments

Medicines for symptomsDonepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine and memantine can help memory and daily function a little for a while in some people. They do not change the course of the disease.
Newer Alzheimer's medicinesLecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla) remove amyloid, a protein that builds up in the brain in Alzheimer's. In studies they slowed decline by roughly a quarter to a third. They are only for early Alzheimer's with confirmed amyloid.
The trade-offsThey can cause brain swelling or small bleeds, so you need regular MRI scans, and the risk is higher with a gene called APOE4. Leqembi can now be given as a weekly shot at home instead of infusions. Medicare covers both for people who qualify.
Living well with dementiaExercise, social connection, treating hearing loss, good sleep, and support for caregivers all improve daily life, whatever the cause.
07

Help for caregivers

Caring for someone with dementia is hard, and you do not have to do it alone. Pick what you need help with.

Tell us how you are doing, too. Caregivers have higher rates of depression, poor sleep and health problems of their own. Taking care of yourself is part of taking care of them.

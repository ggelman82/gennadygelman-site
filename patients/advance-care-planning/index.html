Planning ahead

Advance care planning

Who speaks for you if you cannot speak for yourself? Picking that person today is a gift to your family. Here is what I tell my patients about the health care proxy, your wishes, and the MOLST form in Massachusetts.

Who
Every adult, starting at age 18
What
Name one person to speak for you
Cost
Free. No lawyer or notary needed.
Where
At your next visit. We can be your witnesses.

Key points

Name a health care agent.One person you trust, plus a backup. Every adult should do this.
Tell them what matters to you.The form names a person. It does not say what you want. Have the talk.

01

Why do this now.

When you turn 18, your parents no longer make your medical decisions. From that day on, it is good to name someone who can decide for you if you cannot speak for yourself. Accidents and sudden illness happen at every age.

Many people think a husband, wife or adult child can step in. In Massachusetts that is not automatic. There is no legal list of family members who get to decide. If you have not named anyone, and a serious choice comes up or your family disagrees, a judge may have to appoint a guardian.

I think choosing someone now is a gift to your family. Families often disagree about what a loved one would want. That is hard at the very moment they should be supporting each other. When you name your person today, you spare them that decision. You are saying: I care about my family, so I am making this choice now.

As long as you can speak for yourself, none of this is used. You make your own choices.

This page is about Massachusetts. Other states use different forms and rules.

03

The health care proxy.

The form lets you name a health care agent. That is the person who speaks for you. You can also name an alternate, who steps in if your agent cannot be reached.

When it is used

Your agent decides for you only after your doctor writes that you cannot make or share your own choices. If you get better, you decide again. While you can speak for yourself, your word always comes first.

How to pick your agent

  • Someone who knows you and what matters to you
  • Someone who can speak up for you, even when others disagree
  • Someone your care team can reach quickly
  • Someone you have asked, and who said yes

You can name anyone you want. Name one agent and one alternate, not two people to share the job. Do not pick your own doctor.

Pick an alternate who is a little more distant. Many people name a spouse or close family member as their agent. But a car crash or a house fire can hurt both of you at once. So I ask that your alternate be someone who is less likely to be in the same place as you.

Tell them. Always tell your agent and your alternate that you picked them. Nobody should be surprised later, and they need to know what you want.

How to sign it

You sign the form in front of two adult witnesses. Your agent and your alternate cannot be your witnesses. Anyone else can. You do not need a lawyer or a notary.

We are happy to be your witnesses. You can fill out the form at your next visit. Tap the button so it is on your list.

Where the form goes

  • We scan it into your medical record, so your care team can always find it.
  • We give you a copy.
  • We can make copies for your agent and alternate. That way they can show they are your agent.
  • Keep the original at home where it is easy to find. Do not lock it in a safe deposit box.

Changing your mind

You can change your proxy at any time. You cannot edit the old form. You fill out a new one, with two witnesses, and the newest form replaces the old one. If your agent is your spouse and you divorce or legally separate, the proxy ends on its own. Look at your form once a year to be sure the names and phone numbers are still right.

04

Tell your agent what matters.

The proxy form only names a person. It does not tell that person what to do. If they have to guess, they may carry guilt about choices you never wanted. So talk with them, and write your wishes down.

You do not need to list medical details. Nobody needs to know which needle or which machine. What helps is knowing what kind of life would be okay with you, and what would not.

  • If a stroke left you unable to swallow, would you want a feeding tube?
  • If you could not speak, but could answer by tapping or moving your eyes, would that be okay?
  • If you could no longer walk, or needed care around the clock?
  • Is there a point where you would rather not be kept alive?

Talking about dying is uncomfortable for many people. That is normal. Two books that help: Staring at the Sun by Irvin Yalom, and Being Mortal by Atul Gawande.

Five Wishes

I like a booklet called Five Wishes. It walks you through who should decide for you, what care you want, how you want to be treated, and what you want your loved ones to know. There is a paper version and an online version.

It does not cost much. As of late 2026, the paper booklets are about $2 each when you buy 10, and $1 each when you buy 25 or more. I think they make a great holiday stocking stuffer. They matter more than a scratch ticket.

One caution. In Massachusetts, a living will such as Five Wishes guides your agent, but it is not legally binding by itself. If you have not named a health care agent, nobody has the legal power to carry out your wishes. Do both: the proxy and the wishes.

05

If you travel or live in two states.

Many of my patients spend the winter in Florida or another state. Each state has its own forms and laws.

  • Your Massachusetts proxy will probably be honored in other states, but that is not a promise.
  • If you spend a lot of time in another state, fill out that state’s form too.
  • Five Wishes is written to be used across the country, so it is a good fit for snowbirds. Keep your Massachusetts proxy as well.
  • Carry a copy of your proxy when you travel.
06

MOLST: for serious illness or frailty.

Some people already know there are treatments they do not want. I am happy to have that talk with you, and to help you decide when it makes sense.

MOLST stands for Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment. It is a medical order that you and your clinician sign together. It is for people with a serious illness or frailty. It is not for everyone.

What CPR can and cannot do

CPR helps some people far more than others. A young, healthy person whose heart stops from a sudden, fixable cause has a much better chance of going home and getting on with life. In an older person with a worn-out heart or a serious illness, the chance is much lower, even with CPR, shocks to the heart and intensive care.

12 to 19out of 100 people age 70 or older leave the hospital alive after CPR for a cardiac arrest in the hospital. The number falls with age. It is lower still when the heart stops outside a hospital.

CPR may get you to the hospital. It does not mean you will leave the hospital. It can also mean broken ribs, breathing tubes and days of discomfort before dying there anyway. So we should talk about how you are doing day to day, how frail you are, and what your real odds are.

Some people with a terminal illness go through all of this only to return to a short time left. Many of them tell me: if my heart stops, let me go.

What the form covers

The MOLST tells emergency crews and hospitals what you do and do not want:

  • CPR, which includes chest compressions and shocks to the heart
  • A breathing tube and a breathing machine
  • A breathing mask such as CPAP or BiPAP
  • Being taken to the hospital
  • Dialysis
  • Food through a tube
  • Fluids through an IV or a tube

The form is usually printed on bright pink paper so it is easy to spot. Copies are valid. Keep it where a crew can find it, such as on the refrigerator or by your bed, and take a copy when you leave home.

If you want everything done, you do not need a MOLST. Full treatment is what emergency crews and hospitals do when there is no order. The MOLST is for writing down the treatments you do not want.

The form is changing its name. Massachusetts plans to switch from MOLST to a newer form called POLST in spring 2027. A MOLST you already have stays valid.

Hospice is something different. Hospice is care focused on comfort near the end of life. You can have a MOLST without being in hospice.

07

Be ready for an emergency.

If you land in an emergency room that cannot see your records, a one-page summary tells them who you are and what your story is. Every patient should keep a list of:

  • The clinicians you see regularly
  • Your medicines and doses
  • Your allergies
  • Your health conditions
  • Surgeries and hospital stays
  • Any implants or devices, such as a pacemaker or a joint replacement
  • Emergency contacts, and who your health care agent is

File of Life

The File of Life is a bright red magnetic pocket that goes on your refrigerator. It holds your health summary, and you can add a copy of your proxy and your MOLST. Emergency crews know to look for it and bring it with you to the hospital. There is a small one for your wallet too. Some kits include a sticker for your front door.

Many town senior centers and fire departments in Massachusetts give them out for free. Use a pencil, and update it when your medicines change.

08

Organ and tissue donation.

This is a good time to decide if you want to be a donor. In Massachusetts you can sign up online at RegisterMe.org, or check the box at the Registry of Motor Vehicles when you get or renew your license or ID. Tell your agent and your family what you chose.

09

When you need a lawyer.

You do not need a lawyer for a health care proxy. A lawyer does help with:

  • A durable power of attorney for money and legal matters
  • A will or estate plan
  • Guardianship, when someone can no longer make their own decisions and never named an agent
  • Family situations that are complicated

Ask us for a referral. My practice works with a medical-legal partnership in Worcester, run with Community Legal Aid. We can refer any patient. If you do not qualify for their free services, they can point you to a local lawyer for what you need.

10

What to do next.

  • Pick your agent and your alternate. Ask them.
  • Talk with them about what matters to you.
  • Sign the proxy with two witnesses. We can do this at your visit.
  • Write your wishes down, with Five Wishes or in your own words.
  • Give copies to your agent, your alternate and your care team.
  • Keep a health summary where it is easy to find.
  • Seriously ill or frail? Ask me about a MOLST.

Parents: when your child turns 18, help them sign a proxy too. Without one, you may not be able to make decisions for them in an emergency.

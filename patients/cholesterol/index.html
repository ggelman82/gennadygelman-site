For my patients

Cholesterol

What your numbers mean, what goal I set for you and why, how plaque forms, and what actually works to lower your risk of a heart attack or stroke.

Key points

  • Lower LDL for longer means fewer heart attacks and strokes. LDL is the number that matters most.
  • Your goal depends on your risk. Under 100, under 70 or under 55. Find yours below.
  • Food and movement come first. Less saturated fat, much more fiber, and regular exercise.
  • If that is not enough, statins work, and most people tolerate them well. There are good options if they don't.

03

What cholesterol is, and why you need it

Cholesterol is not the enemy. Every cell in your body needs it. It is part of every cell wall, and your body uses it to make hormones such as estrogen, testosterone and cortisol, along with vitamin D and the bile that digests fat.

Most of your cholesterol is made by your own liver. That is why eating cholesterol (like eggs) matters less than people think. What raises your blood cholesterol most is saturated fat: cheese, red meat, butter and other high-fat animal products.

What about the brain? The brain makes its own cholesterol. The cholesterol in your blood can't cross into it, so lowering blood cholesterol does not starve your brain. Large studies have not shown that statins harm memory. More about brain health on my memory page.

04

LDL and HDL: the delivery trucks

Cholesterol doesn't float in your blood on its own. It rides in carriers called lipoproteins. When we say LDL and HDL, we really mean those carriers.

LDL, the "bad" cholesterolCarries cholesterol from the liver out to the body. When there is more than the body needs, LDL particles slip into artery walls and build plaque.
HDL, the "good" cholesterolCollects extra cholesterol from the body and brings it back to the liver. Exercise raises it.
The ratioTotal cholesterol divided by HDL. About 3 or less is good. At 5 or more, for every five trucks going out only one comes back, and the extra has to go somewhere.
TriglyceridesAnother blood fat, raised mostly by sugar, refined carbs, alcohol and extra weight. Very high levels can also inflame the pancreas.
05

How plaque forms

Plaque is a buildup of cholesterol and inflammation inside the artery wall. Over years it does two things: it narrows the artery, and it can crack, so a clot forms on top and blocks it. Either way, tissue downstream doesn't get enough oxygen. In the heart, that is a heart attack. In the brain, a stroke.

What drives it:

LDL, on its ownLDL is enough to cause plaque by itself. The higher it is and the more years it stays high, the more plaque builds. That is why lowering it early pays off.
Damage to the artery wallHigh blood pressure, diabetes and smoking injure the inside of the arteries, so plaque builds faster.
Inflammation, the "stickiness"I think of inflammation as how sticky things are. A blood test called high-sensitivity CRP measures it. High numbers plus high stickiness is the worst combination.
Your genesLp(a), a cholesterol particle you inherit, makes plaque and clots more likely. Family history of early heart disease matters too.
06

The tests I use

PREVENT risk scoreThe newest calculator, recommended in the 2026 guideline. It estimates your chance of a heart attack, stroke or heart failure over the next 10 and 30 years, using your numbers, blood pressure, kidney function, blood sugar and smoking.
Lp(a), once in your lifeLp(a) is mostly genetic and barely changes with diet, so one test is usually enough. Since March 2026 the national guideline says every adult should have it checked once. A high level, 125 nmol/L or more, raises long-term risk about 1.4 times; 250 or more, about double. I show you your risk score both with and without it.
Direct LDLMost labs calculate LDL from your other numbers, which can be off, especially if your triglycerides are high or you ate recently. I like to also measure it directly.
ApoBCounts the actual number of plaque-causing particles. It helps most with diabetes, high triglycerides or very low LDL, when the usual numbers can understate risk.
High-sensitivity CRPThe "stickiness" test. A level of 2 or more points to inflammation that adds to risk.
Coronary calcium scoreA quick, low-dose CT scan of the heart that shows calcified plaque. Useful when we are unsure whether to start a statin. A score of zero is reassuring; any calcium means plaque is already there.

07

Food and exercise

I'd rather every patient improve cholesterol with diet and exercise. Good food mostly lowers LDL. Exercise mostly raises HDL and lowers triglycerides.

Eat more fiberFiber helps your liver dump cholesterol into your gut, where it leaves in your stool. Oats, beans, lentils, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Fiber also lowers colon cancer risk and feeds healthy gut bacteria.
Cut back on saturated fatCheese, red and processed meat, butter, cream and fried food raise LDL the most. Swap in olive oil, nuts, fish and beans.
Go easy on sugar and refined carbsWhite bread, sweets, soda and juice raise triglycerides and lower HDL.
Move most days150 minutes a week of brisk walking or similar activity raises HDL. Strength work twice a week helps too.
Lose a little weight, if you need toEven 5% to 10% helps. Try the BMI calculator on my weight loss page.
Don't smoke, and limit alcoholSmoking damages arteries and lowers HDL. Alcohol raises triglycerides.
08

Medicines

After years of risk factors, diet and exercise alone often can't get LDL to goal. That is where medicine comes in. Lowering LDL with medicine prevents heart attacks and strokes, not just better numbers on paper.

StatinsThe first choice. Atorvastatin, rosuvastatin and others block an enzyme (HMG-CoA reductase) the liver uses to make cholesterol. They lower LDL by 30% to 50% or more and have decades of proof that they prevent heart attacks, strokes and death.
Side effects, honestlyMuscle aches are the most common worry. But in studies where neither patients nor doctors knew who got the statin, people on dummy pills reported almost as many aches. Most aches blamed on statins are not caused by them. Real muscle or liver problems are uncommon, and switching statins or lowering the dose usually fixes them.
EzetimibeA pill that blocks cholesterol absorption in the gut. Added to a statin, it lowers LDL another 20% or so.
PCSK9 inhibitorsStrong LDL lowering, by about 50% to 60%. Most are shots every 2 weeks or monthly. The first pill in this family, enlicitide (Lipfendra), was approved in July 2026.
On the horizon: one-time gene editingIn a 2026 study of 35 people with inherited high cholesterol or early heart disease, a single infusion of VERVE-102 permanently switched off a cholesterol gene in the liver. At the highest dose, LDL fell by an average of 62%, and the effect has lasted up to 18 months so far. It uses base editing, a newer form of the Nobel Prize-winning CRISPR technology. It is still experimental, with larger trials starting now, but I am excited about it.

09

Things you may have heard

Better than nothingRed yeast rice

It contains a natural statin, the same molecule as lovastatin, in smaller and less predictable amounts. A statin is better proven and you know exactly what dose you're getting. But if you don't want a statin, I'll support you taking red yeast rice rather than nothing. Pick a brand tested by USP or NSF, since some products contain a kidney toxin called citrinin, and don't take it with a statin.

Small effectGarlic

There is some evidence it lowers cholesterol a little. Enjoy it in food, but it is not a replacement for medicine.

I recommend againstNiacin

It improves the numbers, but large trials showed it did not prevent heart attacks or strokes, and it caused side effects such as flushing, higher blood sugar and bleeding.

I recommend againstFish oil supplements

In the STRENGTH trial, high-dose fish oil was no better than corn oil at preventing heart attacks and strokes, and it caused more atrial fibrillation, an irregular heartbeat. One prescription form, icosapent ethyl (Vascepa), lowered events in another trial for some people with high triglycerides already on a statin. Eating fish is still great.

Depends on youDaily aspirin

If you have had a heart attack, stroke, stent or bypass (secondary prevention), aspirin is usually right for you. If you haven't (primary prevention), most people should not take it: the bleeding risk, from the stomach or the brain after a fall, usually outweighs the benefit. With diabetes between 40 and 75, it may make sense if your heart risk is high and bleeding risk is low. Don't start or stop it without talking with us.

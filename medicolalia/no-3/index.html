Medicolalia · Radically examining medical terminology · No. 3

Seeds of Disease

By Gennady Gelman, MD

As winter approaches, we spend more and more time talking about germs. In the exam room, we often explain that a patient’s upper respiratory illness is viral, not bacterial. Then we gently remind them that several of the infections we worry about can be prevented with vaccines. Germs, viruses, bacteria, vaccines—the words are familiar, but their histories are less so. They tell the story of how we came to understand illness and, in some cases, learned to prevent it.

A pox on your houses

Smallpox had afflicted people for thousands of years. The roughly 3,000-year-old mummy of Ramses V bears skin lesions consistent with the disease, though the diagnosis remains uncertain. Written descriptions appear in fourth-century CE China.

In English, a pock is a pustule or the pit or scar it leaves behind. The word comes from Old English pocc, meaning a pustule or blister. Its plural, pocks, came to be spelled pox: the letter x does the work of cks. New Englanders would be quick to recognize this with the Red Sox. Thus we arrive at smallpox.

Medicine is full of pairings. Anterior has posterior. And lesser has greater. So if there was a smallpox, what was the big one? The great pox was how the English knew syphilis. That’s what the “small” in smallpox was there to distinguish it from. Other names followed: cowpox, chickenpox, and what we now call mpox. Sharing pox doesn’t necessarily mean sharing a cause. Smallpox, cowpox, and mpox are caused by closely related orthopoxviruses. Chickenpox comes from a herpesvirus, while the “great pox,” syphilis, is caused by a bacterium.

Perhaps you recall thinking that a pox was more of a plague or a curse and not a skin condition? Perhaps it is because you remember Mercutio putting a pox on both houses. Open a modern edition of Romeo and Juliet and there he is, bleeding to death, saying “A plague o’ both your houses!” Before anyone corrects your Shakespeare, though, ask which edition they own. In the first printing, in 1597, Mercutio says “A poxe of your houses.”

The first defense against smallpox grew from an old observation: someone who survived smallpox could nurse others through the next epidemic. In parts of Asia and Africa, people deliberately exposed the susceptible to material from smallpox lesions, hoping to gain that protection. This was variolation. Its root, variola, had been used for smallpox since the sixth century. The word may come from Latin varus, “pimple,” or varius, “speckled.” Either way, the name points to what the disease did to the skin. The hope was to get through a controlled exposure and emerge protected. The danger was that the infection could escape anyone’s control: you could develop severe smallpox, die of it, or pass it to somebody else. But the risk of dying was generally lower than if you caught smallpox naturally. Tough decision either way.

By 1716, an enslaved African known to us as Onesimus had explained the practice to Cotton Mather, the Boston minister who enslaved him. Mather would then promote variolation during Boston’s smallpox epidemic of 1721. We call him Onesimus because his original name is unknown. Mather gave him the name Onesimus, after an enslaved man in the New Testament’s letter to Philemon. The name comes from Greek for “useful.” Mather faced intense backlash, including an attempted bombing of his house, from both the public and medical establishment for promoting variolation, which Boston’s own numbers vindicated: 2 percent of the inoculated died, against roughly 15 percent of those who caught it naturally. The note tied to the bomb ended, “with a Pox to you.”

And if Cotton Mather’s sounds familiar it may be because his name reappears occasionally in popular culture. He strongly believed in witchcraft and his 1689 book Memorable Providences probably helped spark the initial panic in Salem. After the Salem witch trials, during which he didn’t sit as a judge but is often remembered that way, he did defend the court’s actions.

And that first name, Cotton? An enslaver bearing that name might make you think of the crop that slavery later made king. But Mather was named for his maternal grandfather, John Cotton. The English surname traces to places whose names meant “at the cottages or huts.” The cotton in your shirt has a different history. English cotton reached us through French from Arabic quṭn.

Around the same time, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu saw the practice in the Ottoman Empire and had her young son inoculated there. These were separate routes by which an existing body of knowledge reached an English-speaking audience. Vaccination would grow out of a world already familiar with the idea of deliberately introducing an infection to prevent a worse one.

Benjamin Franklin wrote about variolation—then commonly called inoculation—in his autobiography: “In 1736 I lost one of my sons, a fine boy of four years old, by the small-pox, taken in the common way. I long regretted bitterly, and still regret that I had not given it to him by inoculation. This I mention for the sake of parents who omit that operation, on the supposition that they should never forgive themselves if a child died under it; my example showing that the regret may be the same either way, and that, therefore, the safer should be chosen.”

In Latin, inoculare meant to graft a bud onto a plant. It contains oculus, which meant an eye but could also mean a bud. We still call the buds on a potato its “eyes”: leave one long enough and it sprouts. Picture a gardener slipping a single bud under the bark of a fruit tree. Then picture an inoculator introducing material through a scratch in the skin. That little planted eye is what medicine borrowed when it borrowed inoculation.

Today we’re more likely to picture a syringe doing the job. The word comes from Greek, where syrinx meant a hollow pipe or reed. In a Greek myth, Syrinx was a nymph transformed into reeds while fleeing Pan, who fashioned the hollow reeds into his panpipes, named after himself.

The milkmaid and the gardener

In the late 1700s, smallpox was a familiar threat in England, but on the dairy farms of Gloucestershire, in southwest England, people knew another kind of pox. Cows sometimes developed sores on their udders, and the people who milked them could develop sores on their hands.

Today we know that cowpox is caused by a virus whose natural reservoir is wild rodents, including mice and voles. It occasionally infects other animals, including cattle and cats. People can become infected through a break in the skin, and milking provided a regular opportunity for that to happen.

Edward Jenner was a country doctor in Gloucestershire. He and others had observed that people who had recovered from cowpox seemed to be protected against smallpox.

In May of 1796, a milkmaid named Sarah Nelmes had a cowpox sore on her hand. She’d caught it from a cow named Blossom. On May 14, Jenner took material from Nelmes’ sore and put it into scratches on the arm of James Phipps, the eight-year-old son of his gardener. Weeks later, he deliberately exposed the boy to smallpox by variolation. Fortunately, James remained well. No record tells us what he or his father, who worked for Jenner, was asked.

Jenner published his findings in 1798 under a title containing the words Variolae Vaccinae, or “cow smallpox.” Today we can explain the protection in terms Jenner could not: an immune response to cowpox could also recognize and defend against its dangerous relative, smallpox.

Latin vacca meant a female cow—in our story, specifically a dairy cow. Spanish kept it as vaca, which gave us vaquero, a cattle herder or cowboy. The vaqueros helped shape the American cowboy tradition. Latin vaccinus, meanwhile, meant “of or belonging to a cow.” There’s the cow in vaccine.

Latin also had bōs, a broader word for cattle that gave us bovine. Bovine applies to cattle of either sex—cows, bulls, and oxen—and, more broadly, to relatives such as buffalo and bison. Cowpox was never limited to dairy cows; they were simply the animals through which milkers commonly encountered it. But vaccination inherited the adjective in Jenner’s name for cowpox, the one derived from vacca. Had the name taken the broader bovine route, perhaps we’d all be getting bovinated.

Little germs

Jenner’s experiment came decades before germ theory became established. But the language was already sprouting. Germ, originally a bud or sprout, is recorded in the sense of “seed of a disease” by 1796—the very year of his experiment. By 1803, a medical review was asking whether vaccination destroyed or neutralized the “germe of the small-pox.” The word was there; the modern understanding was still to come.

Virus was already a medical word in Jenner’s day, but it conjured something considerably messier: poisonous fluids, pus, and foul discharge seeping from ulcers. Latin virus meant poison or venom and could also describe a slimy liquid. It probably shares an ancient Indo-European root with viscous, associated with flowing and oozing. Physicians could speak of infectious virus without knowing precisely what made it infectious.

In 1878, French surgeon Charles-Emmanuel Sédillot coined microbe, combining Greek mikros, “small,” with bios, “life.” He intended “a small living being,” but the combination would suggest “short-lived” in Greek. The older term microzoa, already in print by 1854, combined mikros with zōon, “animal” or “living being,” to describe microscopic animals. Still, microbe stuck. But it’s never too late to right an etymological wrong.

Microscopist Antonie van Leeuwenhoek used the Dutch term dierkens (“little animals”), which became animalcules in English. Similarly, molecule means “little mass,” while corpuscle means “little body”—a name familiar from red and white blood corpuscles.

Animal itself comes from Latin anima, “breath” or “soul.” Its ancient Indo-European root, meaning “to breathe,” also survives in Sanskrit an, which contributes to prāṇa, “breath” or “vital breath”—a familiar word to anyone who practices yoga.

Bacterium comes from Greek baktērion, “little rod or staff”—the kind you walk with, not the people you employ. Bacteria, however, come in many shapes. Bacillus is Latin for “little rod,” a diminutive of baculum, “rod” or “staff.” We use bacilli to describe rod-shaped bacteria—rod-shaped little rods. Rounded bacteria are cocci, from Greek kokkos, “grain” or “berry.”

In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed mold growing on a plate of staphylococci in his laboratory. Around the mold was an area where the bacteria had failed to grow. The mold was identified as Penicillium, named from Latin penicillus, “little brush,” for its brushlike spore-bearing structures. Fleming named its antibacterial extract penicillin.

The prepared mind

In 1854, Louis Pasteur told an audience in France, “In the fields of observation chance favors only the prepared mind.” Jenner had worked with an observation already familiar in dairy country. He pursued that observation, tested it, and published what he found.

In 1881, Pasteur proposed extending vaccination to other protective inoculations in Jenner’s honor. Four years later, Pasteur’s team treated a boy named Joseph Meister after a rabid dog bit him. Rabies comes from Latin for rage or madness. The virus takes time to reach the brain, giving an unusual opportunity after a bite to administer a vaccine.

What changed after vaccination

Vaccination’s impact can now be measured in lifetimes. A 2024 modelling study estimated that vaccination against 14 pathogens prevented 154 million deaths worldwide between 1974 and 2024, including 101 million deaths in infants. Vaccines weren’t the only reason survival improved: clean water, sewage disposal, oral rehydration, and antibiotics all did their part. But one disease is simply gone. The last naturally acquired case of smallpox occurred in Somalia in 1977. In 1980 the World Health Assembly declared it eradicated.

And now every autumn, when we talk about flu, COVID and RSV, whether we know it or not, somewhere in the conversation we’ll still reference a cow.

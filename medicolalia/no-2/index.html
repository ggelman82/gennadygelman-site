Medicolalia · Radically examining medical terminology · No. 2

Things That Run Together

By Gennady Gelman, MD

In the last post, we met prodrome and postdrome in migraine: the things that run before an attack and the things that run after it. Now let’s look at the things that run together.

That is a syndrome. Greek syndromē joins syn-, together, with the dromos we already know as running. Findings turn up in each other’s company often enough that we stop treating them as coincidence and start treating them as a pattern.

We usually describe a syndrome as a constellation of signs and symptoms. Because this is an etymology series, we cannot use that word without opening it.

Constellation is Latin: con-, together or with, and stella, star. Stars together. It is the same idea in a different language. Greek says syn- and Latin says con-; one gathers runnings, the other gathers stars. They do the same job, and it is worth learning to hear them that way.

A quick tangent. Solar is Latin sol, sun; stellar is stella, star, the same star as in constellation. Our sun is a star named Sol, so every solar panel is, strictly speaking, stellar. I propose we start calling it stellar energy. But I might be alone in this.

Back to our constellation. If a syndrome is a constellation, what are the stars?

The usual answer is signs and symptoms. Signs are the objective findings. Symptoms are the subjective ones. But before we get too far ahead of ourselves, let’s look at those words.

Symptom comes from Greek symptōma, a happening, something that befalls a person. Behind it is sympiptein, to fall together: piptein, to fall, and syn- once more, this time wearing an m. Synptom is hard to say, so the n bends toward the p that follows it. Linguists call this assimilation: ad-, toward, and similis, like, so to make like. The word does it to itself, since adsimilare became assimilare. It is the same prefix as in syndrome, caught twice in two words: running together and falling together.

That falling root is one we already use at the bedside. In ptosis, something droops or descends: the eyelid in blepharoptosis, breast tissue in mammary ptosis. A symptom does not require anything to physically fall. It is the part of illness that happens to someone, which is why it landed on the side of what the patient experiences and reports.

Sign looks like plain English and is Latin in plain sight: signum, a mark, token, or indication. A sign is a mark that someone reads. Its Greek counterpart is sēmeion, which gives us semiotics and semiology, the study of signs and what they mean. A clinician taking a history and examining a patient is doing semiology on a person.

Notice the pattern from the opening repeating itself. Latin signum, Greek sēmeion: so much of medicine is duplicated thusly.

So, symptoms are subjective and signs are objective. But what do we mean here?

Subject and object do not sound like words with roots we would recognize. But you use the root every time you say eject: e-, out, and -ject, from Latin iacere, to throw. To eject is to throw out.

Once you hear it, the family is everywhere. Each member is a direction plus a throw:

Inject: throw in

Reject: throw back

Project: throw forward

Interject: throw between

Deject: throw down, which is why a dejected person is downcast

Subject is sub-, under: throw under. Object is ob-, against or in the way of: throw against. The subjective is what lies under, inside the person having the experience. The objective is what is thrown in front of us, where anyone can run into it.

Here philosophy steps in, because these two words have traded places. For the medieval scholastics, and still for Descartes, a thing existed subjectively when it existed in itself, as a real subject. It existed objectively when it existed as an object of thought, in the mind. That is nearly the reverse of how we use them now.

The modern sense of subjective, existing in the mind, is attested from the early 1700s and was popularized by Kant and his contemporaries. Objective followed it across, though the older meaning remained the more familiar one in English until late in that century.

So the throwing never changed direction. Philosophy reassigned which side faces the world. Every time we write an S and an O in a SOAP note, we are using a distinction that took centuries to settle into its present shape.

The distinction concerns only how we obtain the information. It does not rank how real it is. A patient’s pain does not become less real because the clinician cannot feel it. The symptom is thrown under, held inside the patient. The sign is thrown out in front, where it can be read.

Sometimes the things that run together remain a syndrome. Sometimes they run together to form what we call a disease or a disorder. The name usually ends in one of those three words, and they are not saying the same thing. Syndrome we have already obducted. I hope you don’t object to me using such an archaic term; I thought you would deduce from context that it means autopsy. The other two deserve the same treatment.

Disease came through Old French desaise: lack of ease, discomfort, trouble. In English it was a word for how a person felt before it narrowed toward sickness in the late 1300s. That history leaves a small irony. We now speak comfortably of asymptomatic disease: a disease with no dis-ease in it, and nothing that has yet befallen the patient.

Disorder is the most detached of the three: dis- and ordo, order undone. It describes an arrangement, not an experience. One might wonder whether chaos would serve better than disorder. They are similar words, but disorder sounds like a filing problem. Chaos is closer to what a condition does to a person’s life. Ask someone living with an attention or anxiety disorder which word fits their week better.

The Greeks had the word ready. Khaos was not a mess. It was the abyss, that which gapes wide open, the emptiness before there was a world. The sense of utter confusion arrived much later, around 1600.

Its opposite, at least since Ovid, is kosmos: order, good arrangement, and by extension adornment, which is why cosmetic is a relative. Pythagoras is said to have been the first to apply the word to the universe, perhaps meaning the starry firmament.

Which brings us back to where we began. A constellation is a small piece of cosmos: stars that someone arranged into a shape and named. A syndrome is the same act performed on findings. We draw lines between the things that befall a patient and give the shape a name. Naming is how we pull a little order out of the chaos.

It is tempting to rank syndrome, disease, and disorder: syndrome for a pattern we cannot yet explain, disease for one we can. But Alzheimer’s disease, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and Down syndrome do not occupy three levels on a ladder of scientific certainty. The last word in a diagnosis reflects history and convention as much as what we know.

AIDS provides a particularly good counterexample. We know its cause, HIV, yet the word syndrome has survived. If syndrome meant “cause unknown,” we would have renamed it decades ago. We did not. More on that below.

Yet another naming word belongs here: idiopathic. Greek idios means one’s own, private, particular to oneself. Pathos is suffering or disease. An idiopathic disease is a disease of its own.

In older medical usage that meant a primary disease, one arising by itself and not as a consequence of something else. In ours it has come to mean a disease whose cause we cannot name. To a patient it may sound like a finding, but to the clinician it is closer to a confession.

Many people likely hear idiotic inside it, and suspect the word is a quiet insult to the disease, the patient, or the doctor. The suspicion is half right. The two words share a common ancestor, but not a meaning.

Greek idiōtēs was simply the private person: someone who held no office and took no part in public affairs. By extension he was the layman, the one without professional skill. It was not yet an insult, although the Greeks could already use it patronizingly.

Then the word began to fall. Latin idiota was an ordinary person or layman, and in Late Latin an uneducated or ignorant one. Old French kept the ignorance. By the time the word reached English in the early 1300s, it could mean a person incapable of ordinary reasoning. Private, then unskilled, then ignorant, then the slur.

Idiopathic took the other branch of the same root: the privacy, not the insult. The disease is not idiotic. It is private. It keeps its cause to itself.

The same idios gives us idiom, a turn of phrase particular to one language, and idiosyncrasy, which entered English around 1600 as a medical term for an individual’s physical constitution. Open it and an old friend is inside: idios plus synkrasis, a mixing together. One’s own particular blend. We keep being brought back together by the prefix syn-; perhaps it is written in the stars.

Seven syndromes, seven naming lessons

Idiopathic is where a name admits uncertainty. Sometimes we learn more, and a diagnosis acquires a new name. Sometimes we keep the old one. And sometimes naming changes for reasons that have little to do with discovering a mechanism. The following seven syndromes show just how differently these stories unfold.

An eponym connects a name with a person after whom something is named. Greek epōnymos joins epi-, upon or after, with onyma, a form of onoma, name. The name family is familiar once you notice it: anonymous, without a name; pseudonym, a false or assumed name; and synonym, a name together, where syn- turns up yet again.

An eponym can be compact, memorable, and an invitation to history. But a surname says little about anatomy or mechanism. As our understanding changes, we sometimes replace a person’s name with words that describe what we have learned.

Stein–Leventhal

Stein and Leventhal’s 1935 report grouped menstrual abnormalities with enlarged polycystic ovaries, and the condition became Stein–Leventhal syndrome, a name that told most people nothing. It was later renamed polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). Open it up: poly- means many; cyst comes from a Greek word for a sac; ovary comes from Latin ovum, egg. The name was descriptive, but the description was misleading: the ovarian features concern follicles rather than abnormal cysts, and the condition extends far beyond the ovaries.

On May 12, 2026, an international consensus initiative announced a new name: polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), with full implementation planned in the 2028 international guideline. Endocrine means secreting within; metabolic draws attention to the body’s chemical processes. The name no longer asks us to look only at cysts that may not even be present. It asks us to consider a broader endocrine and metabolic condition.

Even a descriptive name is a claim about what we understand. As we learn more, the name has to adapt.

Churg–Strauss

Churg–Strauss syndrome became eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA). That is a mouthful, but it is also a pathology lesson: eosinophilic points to a type of white blood cell; granulomatosis to organized inflammatory collections; and polyangiitis to inflammation of many vessels. Asthma and elevated eosinophils often accompany the disease.

There is even a little history of microscopy in the name. Eosinophils took their name from eosin, the stain their granules take up. Granulum is Latin for a little grain; angi- comes from Greek for vessel. A surname has been traded for a set of biological clues.

And there is a sunrise inside the name: eosin comes from Greek Ēōs, dawn, a reference to its color. Homer’s dawn was rosy-fingered, and so is an eosin stain. The word is older than Greek. Sanskrit has it as Uṣas, the Vedic goddess of dawn; Latin, as Aurora. English kept it as east, the direction dawn comes from, and Easter, possibly named for Ēostre, a dawn goddess of its own. A stain named for dawn helped name a cell, which helped name a disease.

Kartagener

Kartagener syndrome described a memorable trio: chronic sinus disease, bronchiectasis, and situs inversus, the mirror-image arrangement of internal organs. But a surname could not explain why chronic respiratory problems and reversed organ positions should run together.

The answer involved cilia, the microscopic projections whose movement clears mucus and whose developmental functions help establish left and right. Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) describes the broader mechanism: dys-, abnormal; kinesis, movement. The older name remains useful for the particular presentation with situs inversus, but PCD explains why some patients have the lung findings without reversed organs.

Down syndrome

Down syndrome takes its name from John Langdon Down, whose 1866 clinical description recognized a recurring pattern but framed it with mistaken racial assumptions. Only much later did scientists discover the chromosomal cause: extra chromosome 21 material, most often a full third copy.

Trisomy 21 says something that the eponym cannot: tri-, three, and -somy, a chromosome-count construction ultimately tied to Greek sōma, body. Yet Down syndrome has not simply been renamed. Both terms survive because one names a clinical syndrome and the other reveals a common genetic explanation.

Reiter syndrome

One name changed for a less comfortable reason. The old “Reiter syndrome” linked arthritis, urethritis, and conjunctivitis to Hans Reiter, whose involvement in Nazi medical atrocities made continuing the honorific indefensible. That is a moral objection, not merely a desire for a more descriptive term.

Reactive arthritis is also a more useful clinical name. It points toward joint inflammation that follows an infection elsewhere, often in the gastrointestinal or genitourinary tract. The familiar three-part presentation is not required; the new name directs us to ask what preceded the arthritis.

Reactive tells us to look for a relationship between events. Arthritis names inflammation of a joint. This is one case in which clearer language and an ethical correction point in the same direction.

AIDS

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) sounds like a placeholder from an era before we knew what caused it. Yet the cause, HIV, has been established for decades, and AIDS remains a distinct term for advanced HIV infection. In the CDC’s surveillance classification, it corresponds to stage 3, which can be defined by a sufficiently low CD4 count or an AIDS-defining illness. A patient need not be visibly ill to meet that definition.

The old name has historical weight, while the explicit stage tells us more precisely what we mean.

Metabolic syndrome

Metabolic syndrome is the most literal return to our constellation. We draw a line around several findings: increased waist circumference, high blood pressure, elevated triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, and elevated blood glucose. Usually, three of the five are enough to satisfy the clinical definition. They tend to travel together, and together they help identify cardiometabolic risk.

But there is no single germ, gene, or lesion that makes the entire grouping inevitable. Insulin resistance is often implicated; lifestyle, genetics, and other influences matter. The word syndrome is doing exactly the work it was built to do: collecting features that run together even when one explanation cannot encompass every patient.

What the seven tell us

These seven have not all been “renamed,” and that is the point. One name moved from anatomy toward physiology (PMOS). Another became a pathology lesson (EGPA). One old syndrome became a recognizable subset of a larger mechanism (Kartagener). One eponym lives alongside a chromosomal explanation (Down). One lost its honorific for ethical reasons (Reiter). Another name persisted despite an established cause (AIDS). And one remains an intentionally practical constellation (metabolic syndrome). Words preserve a discovery, a hypothesis, a convention, or a moral judgment. They do not necessarily keep pace with knowledge.

The tropic table

A constellation is stars someone arranged into a shape. A syndrome is findings someone arranged into a pattern. But what if we arranged the questions instead?

The periodic table is named for periods — rows where electron shells repeat. The body does not work in periods. It works in axes: chains of steps, each with a direction. A tropic table would be a table of turnings: Greek tropos, a turn. The thyroid axis already speaks this language. TSH is also called thyrotropin, the hormone that turns toward the thyroid; TRH is thyrotropin-releasing hormone. Most findings are a turning, toward excess or toward lack. Leukocytosis turns toward excess. Leukopenia turns toward lack. Every pathway, every step, every direction it can deviate.

Take the thyroid as an example. Draw the pathway: TRH to TSH to T4 to T3 to receptor. Then ask three questions at every step: what if this step runs too fast or too much, too slow or too little, or is missing or breaks entirely? Lay the steps across and the questions down, and you have a grid.

For the thyroid, the grid is nearly full. High TSH with low T4 is primary hypothyroidism. Low TSH with high T4 is primary hyperthyroidism, most often Graves’. High TSH with high T4 is a TSH-secreting tumor. Low TSH with low T4 points upstream. The conversion door — T4 to T3 — has its own name: low T3 syndrome, seen in starvation and critical illness. The receptor door is thyroid hormone resistance: the gland works, the hormones work, but the body cannot hear them.

The grid constrains itself. High TSH and high T4 together is a contradiction unless the tumor door or the receptor door is open. Like a Sudoku, you cannot fill every square independently. The squares talk to each other.

And some squares are empty because we have not looked. The UGT1A1 fast variant clears bilirubin so efficiently it also clears more of irinotecan’s active form. Whether that blunts the drug, nobody has shown. The mechanism is known. The disease is not. The square is there; nobody has walked through the door.

Dmitri Mendeleev’s empty squares predicted elements that did not exist yet. Medicine’s empty squares should predict doors nobody has opened. The table is not a map of what we know. It is a map of what we have not asked.

The name is where understanding starts. It should not be where it stops.
